Infrastructure cost optimisation: summary of all plans

Three fully costed routes from the current run-rate of ₹84,800 per month, in addition to the ₹44,000 per month already banked under Phase 0. Plan A re-shapes the platform on Oracle Cloud at approximately ₹21,000 per month. Plan B relocates it to Google Cloud Run in Mumbai at ₹44,000 as a lift-and-shift, or ₹26,000 only after the queue engine has been rewritten. Plan C relocates it to Azure Container Apps in Central India at ₹41,300, or ₹27,000 fully optimised. Every figure traces to a measurement taken from the live estate or to the June 2026 usage statement. The options are presented on their merits first; the recommendation is set out in section 14, after the evidence, the appraisal, the decisions and the risks.

2.1 The rate that decides the outcome

The appraisal reduces to one comparison. The estate is memory-heavy and CPU-light — 44 GiB resident against 1.35 cores in use — so the deciding figure is what a provider charges for a permanently resident unit of capacity. The table prices the nearest available equivalent of one OCI baseline node, 4 OCPU and 24 GB, held warm for a month.

They cannot beat the number that decides the outcome

The workload requires four to five permanently warm cores — authentication, student, the AI and proctoring service and the queue workers — and holds 44 GiB resident. OCI Ampere A1 prices memory at ₹86.82 per GB-month; every alternative screened charges between ₹300 and ₹700 for the same gigabyte. Compute is where the alternatives are closest, at two to three times OCI; memory is where they are five to ten times more expensive, and memory is what this estate consumes.

Serverless pricing only outperforms a low-cost ARM virtual machine below approximately 10% duty cycle. The parts of the estate that are genuinely that idle — DEV, UAT, the builder and batch jobs — can instead be switched off on a schedule at no cost. Practical screening rule for any future proposal: if a provider cannot price memory below roughly ₹150 per GB-month, it cannot reach Plan A, and the remaining modelling is wasted effort.

3 Phase 0 — completed, ₹44,000 per month banked

Common to every option and executed on 17 August 2026. The production database was cut over to pl-prod-pg16 on 31 July, but the preceding PostgreSQL 14 system — two instances of 2 OCPU and 32 GB — continued to run in parallel with nothing connected to it.

Verified before deletion

  • Zero client connections; 2 inserts, 3 updates and 0 deletes across eight days
  • Last row written 3 August, versus 17 August on the live system
  • Data had diverged by 295 students and 5,629 assignment records — a frozen snapshot, never a fallback
  • Zero references in configmaps, secrets, deployments, cronjobs, statefulsets or Grafana across all ten namespaces
  • Post-deletion: all pods running, no new restarts, live database writing normally

Archived, both surviving deletion

  • Logical dump — 2.17 GB, verified by pg_restore -l at 2,603 objects, held in the private bucket pl-prod-db-archive
  • Manual system backup — 1 GB at 35-day retention, covering the four legacy side-databases a logical dump could not read
Correction identified during the work

Three read-only helper scripts had been querying the frozen host since 3 August, silently returning stale production data. All have been repointed.

5 Plan A — re-shape on Oracle Cloud

The platform remains entirely on Oracle Cloud, in Mumbai. The saving derives from matching capacity to measured load, and from switching capacity off when it is genuinely not required.

5.1 Target architecture

  • Production — OKE Basic cluster with a free control plane; two A1 nodes of 4 OCPU and 24 GB always on; one preemptible, calendar-driven burst node at approximately 40 hours per month; self-managed PostgreSQL 16 on A1 4/24 with PgBouncer
  • Frontends — OCI Object Storage behind the existing Flexible Load Balancer
  • DEV and UAT — one virtual machine each with the database merged in, scheduled off overnight and at weekends
  • Batch — parsers and normalisation on OCI Container Instances, billed per second

5.2 Design rules

  • The examination path never sleeps. auth-node, student-node and fast-api remain warm during examination hours; the measured 3.3 s cold versus 8 ms warm is the reason.
  • Application and database stay together. Documented N+1 query patterns turn a 2 ms hop into more than 100 ms per page. If the database moves, the applications move with it.
  • Capacity leads demand. Scaling is driven from the assessment schedule 20 minutes ahead, not from CPU metrics after the fact — producing zero cold start, which is better than serverless.

7 Plan B — Google Cloud Run, asia-south1

A fully serverless target: request-driven containers, a managed database, and no cluster to operate. It satisfies both hard requirements, but its cost depends entirely on whether the queue engine is also rewritten.

The determinant of price — BullMQ

Cloud Run bills a container only while it is handling a request. A queue worker blocking on Redis is not handling a request, and must therefore run with CPU always allocated and min-instances=1 — effectively a virtual machine at approximately twelve times the price. Avoiding that requires rewriting BullMQ to Cloud Tasks or Pub/Sub push so that workers become request-driven, which alters the retry and back-off semantics on which assessment scoring depends.

8.2 Two structural differences from Plan B

  • Container Apps charges a reduced idle rate. A replica held open by a minimum-replica setting but not serving a request bills at roughly one-eighth of the active vCPU rate. Cloud Run charges the full rate for the same warm instance. That single difference is worth tens of thousands of rupees per month across the always-warm tier.
  • KEDA is built into the platform. The BullMQ workers can scale from zero on Redis queue depth without rewriting the queue engine. Plan C therefore obtains Plan B2's worker saving without Plan B2's four weeks of high-risk rewrite, and without disturbing retry, back-off or delayed-job semantics. This is the strongest argument available for Azure.

  • The database decision cuts both ways. 9.3 Vector databases as a PostgreSQL alternative

Qdrant, Weaviate and Pinecone were assessed as a replacement for, or supplement to, PostgreSQL. Measured in production on 27 August 2026, the entire vector estate is approximately 104,000 embeddings occupying 1.7 GB, inside an 11 GB database that is otherwise students, assessments, assignments and scores.

They cannot replace PostgreSQL

Qdrant, Weaviate and Pinecone are similarity-search engines over embeddings and metadata. They have no joins, no foreign keys and no cross-entity transactions, all three of which the system of record requires. They would be an additional data store, never a substitute. Four findings follow.

  • Cost. pgvector on the existing database costs nothing additional. Pinecone, Qdrant Cloud and Weaviate Cloud each start near $25 per month, roughly ₹2,200 to ₹4,400 — between 10% and 20% of the entire Plan A target, to serve 104,000 vectors.
  • Region. Pinecone serverless has no India region, which fails requirement 1 outright. India availability for Qdrant Cloud and Weaviate Cloud requires verification; self-hosting either in Mumbai is possible but adds a service to operate and patch.
  • Correctness. Ranking is hybrid — taxonomy gating, trigram matching and vector similarity fused in a single query. Separating the vectors means fetching identifiers from the vector store and re-querying PostgreSQL, which removes the ability to gate by family before ranking. That gate is precisely what corrected the documented "nurse resembles accountant" and "product development ranks as sales" failures.
  • Direction of travel. Elasticsearch was recently consolidated into PostgreSQL to remove a data store. Adding a vector store puts one back.

When this would change: at roughly ten million vectors, or when pgvector index build time or recall becomes a measured bottleneck. At 104,000 the estate is two orders of magnitude away. A cheaper improvement is available now — institutes_campuses holds about 16 KB per row, indicating unquantised embeddings; halfvec or binary quantisation would shrink that table substantially with no new service and no migration.

12 Performance guardrails and rollback

Requirement 2 states that performance must be no worse than today. The following acceptance criteria enforce it and apply to both plans. Should a guardrail be breached, the previous node count or instance shape is restored immediately and investigation follows; production is never debugged in a degraded state.

Acceptance criteria for every production change

  • API p95 latency does not worsen by more than 10% against the week-1 baseline
  • Error rate and timeout rate do not increase
  • No sustained node CPU above 70–75% after right-sizing
  • No OOM kills, memory pressure or unexpected pod eviction
  • Database connections remain below 70% of the configured limit
  • Queue delay and assessment processing time do not materially increase
  • Login, assessment, scoring, proctoring and dashboard smoke tests all pass
  • Every capacity change has a written, tested rollback before it begins

Observation windows

  • 48 hours after a node drain
  • Seven days after the database cutover
  • 48 hours before deleting any replaced resource

What does not change under either plan

  • No reduction in production replica counts for customer-facing APIs
  • The proctoring and examination path stays warm during scheduled windows
  • All data remains in India
  • No production change without a captured baseline, acceptance criteria and a rollback
  • Email, DNS and the observability stack are untouched
Scale-to-zero boundary

KEDA scale-to-zero is applied to idle queue and administrative workers only. It is never applied to login, session, question delivery, autosave, submission, proctoring, the database or the cache. Cost falls only when idle workloads free enough capacity for a node to be removed safely — which is why right-sizing precedes scale-to-zero in the schedule.

14 Recommendation and next steps

Stated after the evidence, the appraisal, the decisions and the risks, so that the reasoning above stands on its own.

Recommendation — approve Plan A

At the measured load, Plan A costs approximately half of the best achievable Cloud Run outcome. It delivers ₹8,488 of monthly saving in week 0 rather than week 10, requires no data migration, and is reversible at every step. Plan B2 reaches near-parity only after a queue-engine rewrite — substantial risk incurred to finish approximately ₹5,000 per month worse off. Plan C is the strongest of the three migration options on engineering risk, because Container Apps bills warm replicas at a reduced idle rate and its built-in KEDA removes the need for any queue rewrite; it nonetheless lands between ₹27,000 and ₹41,300, because memory at ₹690 per GiB against 44 GiB resident sets a floor that no configuration clears. A move should be revisited if traffic becomes genuinely spiky at ten times today's volume, or if the organisation would rather pay a premium than operate infrastructure at all.

Why the numbers point to Plan A

The comparison reduces to a single ratio. An always-on vCPU costs ₹584 per month on OCI Ampere A1 and approximately ₹6,900 per month on Cloud Run in Mumbai — a factor of roughly twelve. The workload requires four to five permanently warm cores (auth, student, the AI and proctoring service, and the queue workers). Serverless pricing only outperforms a low-cost ARM virtual machine below approximately 10% duty cycle; the parts of the estate that are genuinely that idle — DEV, UAT, the builder and batch jobs — can instead be switched off on a schedule at no cost.

Where Plan B has genuine merit is not cost. Non-production becomes almost free through true scale-to-zero, there is no Kubernetes cluster or database for the team to operate, and automatic on/off behaviour is native rather than assembled from KEDA and a scheduler. Were the binding constraint engineering time rather than money, Plan B would be the stronger answer.
